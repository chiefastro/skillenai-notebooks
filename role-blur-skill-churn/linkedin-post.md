Struts is at 5% of its peak. WebSphere, 6%. Sybase, 4%. SQL Server and Oracle are both below half.

Nobody who built a career on those made a bad call. They were the right choices at the time.

I analyzed 44,417 US tech job postings and 1,029,639 dated career positions going back to 1995, and the result contradicted what I set out to prove.

Skills don't expire. Tools do.

Across the whole vocabulary the median skill is still at 89% of its peak usage, and about half of all skills are still rising. Testing, architecture, requirements gathering: durable. But split out the named technologies, the specific products you list as expertise, and 26% of them sit below a quarter of their peak, versus 4% of everything else. Six times the collapse rate.

The risers are AWS, PyTorch, Databricks and Terraform. The fallers are COBOL, PHP, JSP and SVN.

Two more findings from the same data.

A job title explains under 10% of the variance in the skills a posting actually requires. For 52.5% of postings, most of their closest skill matches are filed under a different title. AI Engineer drifts toward ML Engineer. Cloud, Platform and Infrastructure Engineer all drift toward DevOps. UX Designer sends 51% of its neighborhood to Product Designer, which sends nothing back, a title being absorbed rather than two merging.

And advertised pay rises about 5% per year of required experience right up to 10 years, then stops. Beyond 20 years? 0.03% of postings ask for it. Seven out of 25,392.

So the veteran engineer who feels unhireable is not imagining it, and it is not because his skills went stale. The market priced his first decade and stopped counting.
